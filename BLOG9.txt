Top 5 Key Takeaways
1.	DNS filtering (protective DNS) is the single highest leverage control on this list. It intercepts phishing, malware, command and control traffic before a connection is ever made, and it works regardless of which device, or app tried to make the lookup.
2.	Static blocklists are not enough anymore. Attackers rotate domains in hours, not days. You need behavioral and DGA detection layered on top of reputation feeds, or you're always a step behind.
3.	Encryption (DoH, DoT, DoQ) and filtering are not in conflict. A well architected resolver can encrypt traffic in transit and still apply full policy. The idea that encrypted DNS breaks security is a myth.
4.	DNS security only works if it covers every device, not just the ones on your office network. Roaming laptops, BYOD, IoT, and guest devices are where most DNS security programs quietly fail.
5.	Deployment discipline matters as much as technology. Start in monitoring mode, roll out policy in tiers, integrate logs into your SIEM, and review your exception list every quarter, or the best DNS security stack in the world slowly erodes into basically nothing being blocked.

Why Enterprise DNS Deserves This Much Attention
Let's start with an uncomfortable question. If you asked your IT team right now who is actively watching your organization's DNS traffic, what would they say?
For most enterprises, the honest answer is nobody, or maybe "the resolver our ISP handed us by default." Compare that to how much scrutiny goes into your firewall rules, your endpoint detection platform, or your email security gateway. DNS, despite being older than the web itself and involved in essentially every single online action your organization takes, tends to get whatever configuration shipped with the router.
That's a strange imbalance, because DNS isn't a side character in the attack story. It's usually the opening line. Before ransomware encrypts a single file, before a phishing kit harvests a single password, before a command-and-control channel starts pulling instructions from an attacker's server, there's almost always a domain lookup. "Where is this domain?" That question gets asked billions of times a day, and it happens before the malicious payload ever download.
If you can see and control that question, and the answer your network gets back, you have one of the earliest and cheapest intervention points available in the entire security stack. Most enterprises simply aren't using it. This guide is about fixing that, with 15 specific, deployable practices rather than a vague call to take DNS more seriously.
A quick note before we dive in. None of this is about ripping out your firewall or your EDR platform and replacing it with DNS controls. DNS security is complementary. Your firewall watches IP addresses and ports. Your endpoint tools watch managed devices. DNS security watches something neither of those can fully see, the name resolution that happens before almost anything else, on every device, managed or not. Layer it in, and you close a gap that's been sitting wide open.
Let's get into the fifteenth.

1. Deploy Protective DNS Filtering at the Resolver Level
If you only do one thing on this list, make it this one. Protective DNS, sometimes called DNS filtering or DNS threat intelligence, inspects every lookup your network makes in real time against known bad domains, freshly registered domains with suspicious characteristics, algorithmically generated domains, and whatever categories your policy chooses to block. When a device tries to resolve something dangerous, the resolver simply refuses to answer, or hands back a safe warning page instead of the real address.
From the user's perspective, the malicious link just doesn't load. There are no popup and no alert fatigue, just an interruption that never happens because the threat gets quietly neutralized before a single packet reaches the attacker's infrastructure.
What makes this such a high leverage control is where it sits in the chain. It doesn't matter whether the malicious link arrived by email, SMS, QR code, a compromised website, or a USB drive with a shortcut pointing somewhere it shouldn't. The moment any device on your network tries to resolve that domain, protective DNS has a chance to step in, regardless of delivery mechanism, regardless of whether the device even security has software installed on it.
The catch is that not all protective DNS is created equal. A system relying purely on daily updated reputation feeds will always be a step behind attackers who spin up disposable phishing infrastructure that lives for six hours and vanishes. The systems that keep pace combine reputation data with behavioral signals such as registration age, structural similarity to known brands, and unusual naming patterns, so they can flag a domain within minutes of it going live rather than after it's already been reported by a victim.
When you're deploying this, resist the urge to flip straight to full enforcement. Start in monitoring mode for a couple of weeks. You'll learn two things fast: what your actual baseline DNS traffic looks like (you'll be surprised how many SaaS tools and background services are constantly phoning home), and how much risky traffic is already quietly present. Both inform how aggressively you can enforce policy without a VP's favorite tool suddenly breaking.

2. Turn On DNSSEC Across Every Authoritative Zone
DNSSEC solves a different problem than filtering does. It's about authenticity, not content. It adds cryptographic signatures to your DNS records so that resolvers can verify a response genuinely coming from your authoritative nameserver and wasn't tampered with along the way. That directly counters spoofing and cache poisoning attacks, where an attacker injects a forged record into a resolver's cache so that a user typing the correct URL still gets silently redirected to a malicious server.
This is one of the more commonly skipped best practices, mostly because it has a reputation for being fiddly to set up and maintain: key rotation, signing zones, coordinating with your registrar. That reputation isn't entirely undeserved, but the tooling has improved dramatically, and most modern DNS hosting providers and registrars now offer largely automated DNSSEC signing and rotation.
It's worth being precise about what DNSSEC does and doesn't do, because this is one of the most persistent points of confusion in DNS security conversations. DNSSEC verifies that a response is authentic. It says nothing about whether the destination that response points to is safe. A domain can be perfectly, cryptographically signed and still lead somewhere dangerous. DNSSEC closes one specific door, tampering and spoofing. It doesn't replace protective DNS filtering and treating it as a complete solution on its own is a mistake enterprises make more often than you'd think.
Enable it in every zone you're authoritative for, not just your primary domain. Attackers love forgotten subdomains and legacy zones precisely because they tend to be the ones nobody remembers to lock down.

3. Encrypt DNS in Transit with DoH, DoT, or DoQ
Plaintext DNS queries can be read and, in the wrong network conditions, tampered with by anyone sitting on the path between a device and its resolver. That's not a theoretical risk on public WiFi, hotel networks, or any network your organization doesn't fully control, which, given how much work now happens outside the office, describes quite a lot of networks.
Three protocols solve this by encrypting DNS queries in transit. DNS over HTTPS wraps queries inside standard HTTPS traffic, so they're indistinguishable from ordinary web browsing to an outside observer. DNS over TLS encrypts over a dedicated port, which makes it easier for network administrators to identify and manage specifically, at the cost of being more visible and therefore more blockable by a restrictive network. DNS over QUIC is the newest of the three, built on QUIC transport for faster connection establishment and better performance on lossy or high latency connections.
You don't need to pick exactly one and commit forever. A well-built enterprise DNS security provider supports all three, so your devices aren't locked into a single option depending on network conditions.
Here's the part worth saying directly, because it's a myth that stops a lot of organizations from adopting encrypted DNS: encryption does not break filtering. Encrypting a query in transit protects it from being read or altered by a third party sitting on the network path. It says nothing about what the resolver you've deliberately chosen to send that query to is allowed to do with it. If your devices are pointed at a resolver that both encrypts and filters, you get privacy and protection at the same time, not one at the expense of the other.

4. Lock Down Recursive Resolvers and Kill Open Resolvers for Good
An open DNS resolver is one that answers recursive queries from any source on the internet, not just your own network or authorized clients. Open resolvers are a favorite tool for DNS amplification attacks, where an attacker sends a small, forged query and the resolver reflects a much larger response at a victim, effectively turning your infrastructure into a weapon in someone else's DDoS attack against a third party.
If your organization runs any authoritative or recursive DNS infrastructure of its own, and many enterprises do, even if it's just an internal resolver for corporate devices, audit it specifically for this. Recursive resolution should only be available to your own network ranges and authenticated clients. Response Rate Limiting should be configured to throttle abusive query volumes automatically. And any resolver that doesn't strictly need to be internet facing shouldn't be.
This isn't glamorous work, and it rarely shows up on a roadmap next to something like behavioral threat detection, but an open resolver sitting quietly on your network is exactly the kind of infrastructure debt that turns into a very bad phone call from your ISP's abuse desk, or worse, gets your organization's own infrastructure implicated in an attack against someone else.

5. Implement Response Policy Zones for Real Time Enforcement
Response Policy Zones, or RPZ, are the mechanism that makes real time blocking at the resolver level possible and scalable, rather than something bolted on as an afterthought. RPZ lets a DNS resolver apply custom policy to specific domains: overriding a response for a known bad domain, redirecting the query to a warning page, or simply returning NXDOMAIN (meaning this domain doesn't exist) instead of the real answer.
Think of RPZ as the plumbing underneath protective DNS filtering. The threat intelligence tells the system which domains are dangerous, and RPZ is what enforces that decision now a device asks the question, at scale, across every query your network handles, without meaningfully adding latency.
If you're building or evaluating internal DNS infrastructure rather than relying entirely on a managed provider, confirm your resolvers support RPZ natively, and confirm how quickly policy updates propagate. A policy engine that takes hours to push a new block across your resolver fleet is not meaningfully different from not having one, given how fast modern phishing and malware infrastructure rotates.

6. Log Every DNS Query and Feed It into Your SIEM
DNS query logs are one of the most underrated data sources in enterprise security, and they’re not close. Every device, every app, every background service, and every piece of malware trying to reach a command server generates DNS traffic constantly. If you're logging into it, you have visibility into essentially everything trying to communicate outward from your network, a vantage point most organizations aren't using at all.
The mistake a lot of teams make is treating DNS logs as a separate, siloed thing, living in a standalone console that gets checked occasionally and stays disconnected from everything else the security team is watching. That's a waste of a genuinely valuable data source. Stream DNS query logs into whatever your team already uses for detection, whether that's Splunk, Sentinel, Datadog, or Elastic, so DNS layer signals can correlate with endpoint alerts, identity events, and network traffic instead of sitting in isolation.
This matters for incident response as much as detection. When something goes wrong, DNS logs frequently hold the earliest evidence of what happened: the first lookup to a command-and-control domain, the first sign of a tunneling pattern, or the moment a compromised account started resolving domains it never had before. Investigators without DNS log access are working with a meaningful blind spot, often without realizing it until they need that data and don't have it.

7. Detect Domain Generation Algorithms and Behavioral Anomalies
Modern malware families, especially the ones built for resilience against takedown efforts, frequently use domain generation algorithms to produce large numbers of pseudo random domains on a schedule, sometimes thousands per day. The malware and the attacker's infrastructure both run the same algorithm independently, arriving at the same set of domains without needing a hardcoded command server address that defenders could simply block once and be done with it.
This is a genuinely brutal evasion technique against static defenses, because traditional blocklists rely on already knowing a domain is bad. DGA based malware generates fresh candidates constantly, faster than any manually maintained list can keep pace with.
The only reliable countermeasure is behavioral and statistical detection, recognizing the pattern of algorithmically generated domains rather than trying to catch each individual name. That means watching for unusual entropies in domain strings, characteristic length distributions that don't match how humans name things, and high volumes of NXDOMAIN responses as malware cycles through dozens or hundreds of generated candidates hoping one resolves.
When you're evaluating a DNS security solution, ask specifically about DGA and behavioral detection capability, separate from blocklist matching. This is one of the clearest lines between a DNS security tool that catches modern threats and one that's a well marketed blocklist a few years behind the actual threat landscape.

8. Shut Down DNS Tunneling Before It Becomes an Exfiltration Channel
DNS tunneling is one of the more elegant, and more dangerous, abuses of the protocol, precisely because DNS traffic is almost universally allowed through firewalls. Port 53 stays open on nearly every network on earth, because blocking it breaks the internet for that network. Attackers know this, and they use it as a covert channel.
Here's how it typically plays out in practice. Malware on a compromised machine encodes stolen data such as credentials, documents, or keystrokes into subdomain labels, something like a long string of characters followed by a domain the attacker controls and sends it out as a routine looking DNS query. The attacker's own authoritative server, which they control, receives that query, decodes the embedded data, and can respond with further instructions encoded the same way. To a casual glance, it's just a DNS lookup. To a system analyzing query patterns, it looks like a slow, steady drip of data leaving the building through a door nobody's watching.
Catching this requires looking at query characteristics that a normal DNS lookup simply doesn't have unusually long subdomain labels, high query volume to a single domain in a short window, high entropy in the queried names, and query patterns that don't match how legitimate applications use DNS. A dedicated DNS security platform with tunneling detection built in will flag this automatically. If you're relying on generic network monitoring, this is one of the categories most likely to slip through entirely unnoticed.

9. Restrict Zone Transfers and Lock Down AXFR
Zone transfers, the mechanism secondary nameservers use to replicate a full copy of a DNS zone from a primary server, can hand an attacker your organization's entire DNS map in a single request if they're left unrestricted. That's every subdomain, every internal hostname, and every piece of infrastructure you've ever pointed a DNS record at, served up in one convenient list to anyone who asks.
This should be restricted to explicitly authorized secondary nameservers only, using IP allowlisting at minimum and TSIG (transaction signature) authentication where your infrastructure supports it. It's a genuinely simple fix, and it's also one of those things that's easy to configure correctly once and then completely forget about as infrastructure changes over the following years. New secondaries get added, old ones get decommissioned, and the allowlist quietly drifts out of sync with reality.
Periodically test this from outside your network. Attempt a zone transfer against your public nameservers the way an attacker would and confirm it's refused. If it isn't, you've just found a reconnaissance goldmine sitting open, and it's worth treating that discovery with real urgency.

10. Harden Registrar and Domain Level Security
DNS security conversations tend to focus heavily on resolvers and query traffic, and understandably so, since that's where the day-to-day threats live. But your domain registrar account itself is a single point of failure that, if compromised, bypasses almost everything else on this list entirely. If an attacker gains control of your registrar account, they can repoint your DNS records anywhere they want, and every technical control downstream of that becomes irrelevant.
A few specific things matter here. Enable a registry lock on your most critical domains, which requires an out of band verification step before any DNS or name server change can go through, specifically to prevent exactly this kind of hijacking. Enforce multi factor authentication on every registrar account with administrative access, and audit who has that access, since this is one of those permissions that tends to accumulate over the years and rarely gets pruned. Use WHOIS privacy where your registrar and jurisdiction support it, so your domain ownership details aren't sitting in a public database as reconnaissance material for anyone planning a social engineering attempt against your registrar's support desk.
This category of best practice doesn't get anywhere near the attention it deserves, largely because it feels more like an administrative task than security control. It isn't. Domain hijacking incidents, where an attacker socially engineers a registrar's support team or exploits a weak account into handing over control of a corporate domain, remain a real and recurring threat, and the fix is almost entirely procedural rather than technical.

11. Build Redundant, Segmented Resolver Architecture
A DNS security layer that goes down along with your primary upstream provider isn't protecting anyone. It's just adding a single point of failure to your network's most fundamental function. Real resilience here means a few specific architectural choices.
Anycast routing lets your resolver infrastructure reroute around a failing upstream root or provider in seconds rather than minutes, because multiple geographically distributed instances can answer the same address, and traffic automatically routes to a healthy one. Split horizon DNS, where internal and external queries get different answers for the same domain, prevents your internal infrastructure map from being exposed to the public internet through a misconfigured public facing resolver. And genuine multi provider or multi region redundancy, not just multiple servers that all happen to sit behind the same upstream connection, protects you against the kind of infrastructure scale incident that has, more than once in the past decade, caused an outage at a major DNS provider to ripple into outages across a huge swath of the internet.
The uncomfortable truth about resilience planning is that it's the category of work that only proves its value during an incident, which makes it perpetually easy to deprioritize in favor of something with a more visible weekly return. Don't let it stay deprioritized. Test your failover. Simulate a resolver outage and watch what happens, rather than assuming the runbook works because it exists on paper.

12. Extend Protection to Roaming, Remote, and Unmanaged Devices
This is where a lot of otherwise well-built DNS security programs quietly fail, and it's worth being blunt about it. Protection that only applies while a device sits on the corporate office network offers very little value in a world where a large share of work happens on home Wi-Fi, coffee shop connections, and airport lounges.
A resilient approach applies protection at multiple points simultaneously. On managed devices, that means a roaming client deployed silently through your MDM platform, whether that's Jamf, Intune, Kandji, or Workspace ONE, that follows the laptop wherever it connects, applying the same policy on public Wi-Fi as it would on the corporate network. On the network level, it means protecting everything that isn't running an endpoint agent at all: guest devices, IoT hardware, unmanaged BYOD phones, printers, and even the smart TV in the third-floor conference room, all the quietly present infrastructure that exists on every corporate network and that essentially nobody remembers to secure individually.
If your evaluation of a DNS security vendor doesn't include a specific, direct question about roaming coverage and MDM integration friction, you're missing one of the most consequential factors in whether the deployment actually protects your organization or just protects the subset of it that happens to be sitting in the office that week.

13. Integrate Identity So Policy Follows the User, Not the IP
Traditional network security policy tends to be built around IP addresses and network segments: this subnet gets this policy, that VLAN gets that one. That model breaks down fast in an environment where people move between networks constantly, where IP addresses get reassigned by DHCP, and where the same device might be on the corporate network in the morning and a home network by afternoon.
Syncing your DNS security policy with your identity provider, whether that's Entra ID, Okta, or Google Workspace, through SCIM provisioning and group mapping lets policy travel with the user and the group they belong to, rather than the network segment they happen to be sitting on at a given moment. That means a finance team handling wire transfers can have meaningfully stricter policy than the general office population, and that stricter policy follows finance team members onto whatever network they're connected to, rather than only applying when they're physically in the building.
This also pays off enormously during incident response. When an alert fires, "this user, in this group, triggered this detection" is a dramatically more useful starting point for an investigation than "this IP address, which could theoretically have been any of forty devices that day, triggered this detection."

14. Guard Explicitly Against Spoofing and Cache Poisoning
DNS spoofing, sometimes called cache poisoning, involves injecting false DNS records into a resolver's cache so that a legitimate domain lookup gets silently redirected to a malicious IP address. This is particularly nasty because it defeats the most basic phishing advice anyone's ever been given, which is checking the URL bar. The domain name displayed is correct. It's the destination underneath that's been swapped.
Cache poisoning attacks typically exploit weaknesses in how a resolver validates incoming responses, historically by guessing transaction IDs or source ports before a legitimate response arrives and flooding the resolver with forged replies in that window. Older DNS implementations were especially vulnerable to this class of attack. Modern resolvers have hardened considerably, but poisoning attempts haven't stopped so much as gotten more targeted and sophisticated.
Beyond DNSSEC, which we covered earlier and which directly addresses this by cryptographically verifying response authenticity, a few specific hardening steps matter here. Confirm your resolvers use proper source port and transaction ID randomization rather than predictable sequences. Enabled DNS Cookies where supported, a lightweight mechanism that helps resolvers distinguish legitimate responses from off path forgery attempts. And keep resolver software patched aggressively, since cache poisoning research tends to surface fresh variants against specific implementations on a regular cycle.

15. Plan Your Rollback and Review Exceptions on Real Cadence
The last item on this list isn't technology at all. It's operational discipline, and it's the difference between a DNS security deployment that stays effective for years and one that quietly decays into theater within eighteen months.
Any change to core DNS infrastructure deserves a genuinely tested rollback plan, not just a plan that exists as a paragraph in a document nobody's opened since it was written. What happens if a new resolver has an outage? What's the fallback path? Who has the authority to flip it back, and how fast can they execute that, at two in the morning, without waiting for someone to wake up and approve it? Organizations that skip this step and hit a snag in production tend to develop a lasting, and honestly unfair, grudge against DNS security as a category, when the real gap was a missing runbook rather than a flaw in the approach itself.
The second half of this practice is exception management. Every DNS security deployment accumulates exceptions over time. A domain gets manually allowed because someone needed it for a project last quarter, or a category gets loosened for a team that complained loudly enough. Left unreviewed, that exception list becomes its own quiet form of security debt, and eighteen months in, you can end up with a policy that looks strict on paper but has been effectively hollowed out by accumulated carve outs nobody remembers granting. Set a real cadence. Quarterly works well for most organizations. Review and prune exceptions that are no longer needed. It's unglamorous work, but it's also the work that keeps everything above it on this list meaning something a year from now.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the single most important DNS security best practice for a mid-size enterprise to start with?
Protective DNS filtering at the resolver level, deployed first in monitoring mode. It delivers the broadest, fastest coverage of anything on this list, because it protects every device that resolves a domain through it, and starting in monitoring mode lets you build enforcement policy off real traffic data instead of guesswork.

Is DNSSEC alone enough to secure enterprise DNS?
No. DNSSEC verifies that a DNS response is authentic and hasn't been tampered with. It says nothing about whether the destination that response points to is safe. A domain can be perfectly DNSSEC signed and still be malicious. It's one layer among many, not a complete solution on its own.

Does encrypting DNS traffic with DoH or DoT make filtering impossible?
No, and this is a common misconception. Encryption protects a query from being read or altered by a third party on the network path. It doesn't prevent the resolver you've deliberately chosen to use from applying policy once the query arrives at that resolver. A well architected DNS security provider supports encrypted transport and full filtering at the same time.

How often should we review DNS security exceptions and allowlists?
Quarterly is a reasonable default for most enterprises. Exception lists accumulate steadily as teams request carve out for specific projects or tools, and without a regular review cadence, that list quietly erodes how much protection your policy is providing, even though the policy itself looks unchanged on paper.

Can DNS security stop ransomware before encryption starts?
It can meaningfully disrupt it, though it's rarely the only control standing between an organization and an attack. A large share of ransomware operations relies on DNS at multiple stages, including initial phishing domains for the foothold and DGA based command and control infrastructure to maintain access, and sometimes DNS tunneling for reconnaissance before encryption ever begins. Strong DNS layer visibility frequently catches those precursor stages well before the damage becomes irreversible.

Do small and mid-size enterprises really need this level of DNS security, or is it primarily an enterprise scale concern?
Company size doesn't meaningfully change the threat model here. Attackers running phishing as a service kit and DGA based malware campaigns aren't hand picking targets by headcount. They're casting wide, automated nets. A smaller organization is exactly as reachable by a mass phishing campaign as a large one. The difference usually comes down to which one has any defense at this layer at all.

What's the difference between DNS filtering and full DNS security?
DNS filtering, blocking resolution of known bad or policy violating domains, is one visible feature within the broader discipline of DNS security. The full picture also includes protecting DNS infrastructure itself through DNSSEC, encrypted transport, and resolver hardening, plus using DNS traffic more broadly for threat detection, logging, and incident response visibility.

Bringing the 15 Together
Look back at this list and the pattern should be clear. Enterprise DNS security isn't one control, it's a layered system, and the layers reinforce each other. DNSSEC and encrypted transport protect the integrity of DNS conversation itself. Protective filtering, DGA detection, and tunneling detection catch the threats trying to ride on top of that conversation. Resolver hardening, zone transfer restrictions, and registrar security protect the infrastructure underneath all of it. And logging, identity integration, roaming coverage, and rollback planning are what keep the whole system functioning as your organization changes shape over time, rather than freezing in place as a snapshot of whatever your network looked like on deployment day.
None of this requires tearing out your existing security stack and starting over. It requires pointing your resolvers somewhere that's watching, feeding that visibility into the tools your team already uses, and treating DNS with the seriousness that its role in nearly every modern attack chain genuinely warrants. The internet runs on a question, "Where is this domain?", asked billions of times a second, mostly unwatched. Enterprise DNS security, at its core, is simply the decision to start paying attention to the answer.
